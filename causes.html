Understand the pain.

Possibilities to discuss with a clinician, not a self-diagnosis.

A possibility list, not a ranking

The sources support these as possible causes or aggravating factors. They cannot tell which applies to you, and this is not an exhaustive differential diagnosis. Age, sex and feeling healthy do not establish the cause. [M1] [M2]

First: choose the right level of care

EMERGENCY

Do not wait for exercises or a new chair

Call 911 (US) or your local emergency number for sudden pressure/crushing shoulder pain, especially with chest pain, pain spreading to the jaw, arm or neck, breathlessness, dizziness or sweating. Go to an emergency department after a severe injury with a very painful, swollen, bruised or bleeding shoulder. [M1]

URGENT ASSESSMENT

Seek urgent local medical care

Sudden or very severe pain; inability to move the arm; changed shape or major swelling; persistent pins and needles or loss of feeling; an unusually hot or cold arm/shoulder; pain after an injury or fall; severe pain in both shoulders; fever or feeling unwell. NHS guidance treats these as urgent. [M2]

CLINICAL APPOINTMENT

Worsening or persistent symptoms

Arrange assessment if pain worsens, is not improving after 2 weeks or movement is very difficult. [M2] A newly protruding shoulder blade or weakness deserves a clinician’s examination rather than an assumed “posture problem”; use urgent care for the associated warning signs above. This is the project’s cautious application of AAOS guidance. [M3]

What could explain the symptoms?

Discuss patterns with a clinician; do not use them as diagnostic tests
Possibility What the evidence supports What we cannot conclude
Rotator cuff / tendon or bursa irritation Rotator cuff inflammation, tears, bursitis and overuse/injury of nearby tendons are recognized causes of shoulder pain. Arm use or overhead movement may hurt. [M1] [M2] A painful workout does not distinguish a strain from a tear, and pain alone cannot identify the damaged structure.
Workout overload or nearby tendon injury Overuse or injury of nearby tendons, muscle imbalance and rotator cuff tears are recognized possibilities. A new load, form change or painful repetition can be useful history for a clinician. [M1] Timing after a workout does not identify a strain, tendon problem or tear. Do not test the theory with more painful loading.
Sustained desk work Static postures without breaks can fatigue neck and shoulder muscles. OSHA recommends task variation and short breaks. [W3] Your desk exposure is a plausible contributor, not proof of an injury or the only cause. A posture photo cannot settle this.
Sleep-position aggravation For rotator cuff problems, MedlinePlus suggests the non-painful side or back, with pillows supporting the painful shoulder. [M4] This supports a comfort trial, not the claim that left-side sleeping caused a tear or nerve injury.
Scapular movement disorder / visible winging AAOS describes abnormal shoulder-blade position or movement associated with muscle weakness/tightness, nerve injury or bone/joint injury. Symptoms may include pain, weakness, limited motion, snapping and visible prominence. [M3] “Wing pain” is not itself a diagnosis. A prominent blade does not identify a particular damaged nerve.
Neck or other referred pain Shoulder pain may originate elsewhere, including the neck or lungs. [M1] Do not assume every shoulder-blade symptom is muscular, especially with chest or neurologic symptoms.
Other shoulder conditions Frozen shoulder, arthritis, instability, acromioclavicular-joint problems, fracture and dislocation are other recognized possibilities. [M1] [M2] This list neither confirms nor excludes any of them. Trauma and major loss of function change the urgency.

What an examination can add

A clinician can assess shoulder and shoulder-blade movement, strength and tenderness. Imaging is not always necessary for scapular dyskinesis; it may be ordered for a suspected bone or other shoulder injury. Nerve testing may be considered if nerve injury is suspected. [M3]

Useful details to bring

Project visit-preparation checklist, not a diagnostic questionnaire.

  • When it started; which workout, injury or change preceded it.
  • Where it hurts; whether it spreads; whether sleep is affected.
  • Any visible blade prominence, weakness, numbness or lost movement.
  • What helps or aggravates it; medicines and existing conditions.
