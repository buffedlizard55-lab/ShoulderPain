Section 1 · Medical advice

What could be causing the pain?

Three lifestyle factors line up with your symptoms. The sources below don't confirm any single cause — only a clinician can do that. But they do explain the mechanisms and the signs that separate “overuse” from “something that needs a doctor”.

Sources re-verified September 20, 2026 (pass 3 — every citation re-opened; pass 4 — S1 and S45 re-read in full and confirmed word-for-word)

1. Desk work: 12 hours, slouching, static position

This is the strongest candidate for chronic, low-grade shoulder and wing-blade pain. The mechanism is well documented in occupational safety guidance:

  • Static postures fatigue the neck and shoulder. OSHA's workstation guidance states that “maintaining static postures, such as viewing the monitor, for a prolonged period of time without taking a break can fatigue the muscles of the neck and shoulder that support the head” S16.
  • Forward head and rounded shoulders load the shoulder girdle. Cleveland Clinic explains that poor desk posture (head forward, shoulders rounded, looking down at a screen) overworks the neck and back muscles and is a common driver of neck/shoulder pain and “tech neck” S11. NIOSH defines exactly this kind of problem — musculoskeletal disorders caused by awkward postures, repetitive motion, and sustained force S20.
  • Posture is on the official list of shoulder-pain causes. MedlinePlus (NIH) lists “poor shoulder posture and mechanics” and “imbalance of your shoulder muscles” among the causes of shoulder pain, and its home-care advice includes “practice good posture to keep your shoulder muscles and tendons in their right positions” S1. Mayo Clinic Health System's physical-therapy article on posture and shoulder pain explains the mechanism: the shoulder and back muscles are “all too often … weaker than the muscles in the front of our body due to prolonged sitting, sedentary lifestyles, and frequent and extended use of phones and computers,” and that imbalance “keeps the rotator cuff from working well”; posture changes the positioning of the shoulder blade, how efficiently the shoulder muscles work, and the space available at the shoulder for movement S45.
  • Postural kyphosis (the hunched curve) — a pattern to correct, not the presumed pain source. Cleveland Clinic describes postural kyphosis as the rounded upper back that comes from slouching and says it “doesn't usually cause pain”; “pain or stiffness in your back and shoulder blades” appears in its list of symptoms of more severe kyphosis S12. So the hunch itself is best treated as a posture habit to change (it feeds the imbalance above), not as a diagnosis.
  • Wing-blade mechanics. Physiopedia's winging summary separates musculoskeletal winging — where the muscles that stabilise the blade are weak or poorly conditioned — from nerve-related winging S28, and the physician-published winging handout lists “poor physical conditioning (strength and flexibility)” among the factors that increase risk S27. Combined with the Mayo Clinic Health System point that posture changes shoulder-blade position S45, this is the pattern a 12-hour slouched desk day plausibly feeds. (The exact “chest muscles shorten while rhomboids/serratus lengthen” story is a common clinical explanation but is not spelled out in these sources — treat it as a working model, not an established fact.)
A 10-second self-test from Mayo Clinic Health System “Sitting with slouched posture, raise your arm as high as you can manage. Do you feel tightness and pinching with this motion? Now, sit with your best posture and complete the same motion. Does your motion improve? Does your pain change?” S45 If the tall-sitting version is clearly better, the posture/desk pathway is a strong candidate.
Why 12 hours matters more than your setup Even a perfectly set-up desk becomes a problem at 12 hours of near-static use. OSHA's explicit remedy is task variation: alternate seated postures, take short rest breaks (micro-breaks) to stand, stretch and move, and alternate tasks so different muscle groups rest S16.

2. Sleeping on your left side

Side sleeping is a recognized aggravator for shoulder pain: the body's weight presses on the shoulder joint and the top arm is hard to position comfortably, so it often ends up pulling or compressing the shoulder. The Sleep Foundation's expert-reviewed guide on shoulder pain covers the resulting pillow strategy for side sleepers — including keeping the affected shoulder out of the mattress (sleeping with the sore shoulder up) and using contoured side-sleeper pillows to keep the neck aligned S31. The official (NIH/MedlinePlus) rotator-cuff self-care instructions say the same thing in one line: “When you sleep, lie either on the side that is not in pain or on your back. Resting your painful shoulder on a couple of pillows may help” S2. Cleveland Clinic's neck-pain guidance adds: use a pillow that keeps your head and neck aligned with your body when you sleep on your back or side, and avoid sleeping on your stomach with your head turned S13.

There is also a specific reason left-side sleeping and a sore left shoulder go together: Mayo Clinic Health System describes shoulder impingement as causing pain “with reaching movements, lying on the involved side and changes in the motion of the shoulder” S45 — so the night pain may be a symptom of an irritated shoulder as much as a cause.

If the left shoulder aches on waking and improves as the day goes on, sleep positioning is a leading suspect. Try the low-cost fixes first (sleep on your back or right side; pillow between knees; supportive pillow for the neck) before buying anything — details and options on the Products page.

3. Gym strain / possible injury

If a specific workout (overhead pressing, heavy rows, dips, pull-ups, benching) preceded the pain, or if pain is sharp with certain movements, a soft-tissue injury is plausible. The standard conditions and how they present, per MedlinePlus and Mass General Brigham:

  • Rotator cuff tendinitis / bursitis — inflammation of the cuff tendons or the fluid sac (bursa) under the shoulder; pain when lifting the arm, overhead work, or lying on the shoulder. The most common cause of shoulder pain S1 S10.
  • Muscle strains — overworked or overstretched muscles; one of Mayo Clinic's listed causes of shoulder pain S8.
  • Rotator cuff tears — a tendon partially tears or separates from the bone; can come from a single high-impact event (like falling on the arm) or wear over time. Watch for notable weakness, trouble lifting the arm, or pain that is worse at night S10 S1 S22.
  • Impingement — MedlinePlus describes the most common shoulder-pain mechanism as rotator cuff tendons that “become trapped under the bony area in the shoulder” S1; Mayo Clinic lists impingement among the causes of shoulder pain S8. Mayo Clinic Health System's list of common causes of impingement is worth reading against your own history: “repetitive overhead movement, compression to the shoulder with a fall or other trauma, osteoarthritis, weakness, poor posture” S45 — overhead gym work and desk posture are both on it.

Physiopedia also lists body building/weight lifting among the sports in which scapular winging has been reported S28, and MedlinePlus lists “nerve injury that leads to abnormal shoulder movement” as a cause of shoulder pain S1 — relevant if the “wing” appeared after a specific heavy session (see section 4).

⚑ Flag for review I can't tell from your description whether a specific lifting event triggered the pain. If the pain started suddenly after a particular set/exercise, or you heard/felt a pop, that changes the plan — get evaluated (see when to see a doctor) rather than running the exercise program at full intensity.

4. “Wing” — what scapular winging actually is

Your “wing” sensation most likely refers to the shoulder blade (scapula) sticking out or lifting off the rib cage. Two very different categories exist, and the distinction determines treatment:

Winging vs. dyskinesis (movement pattern) — simplified from clinical references
PatternWhat it meansTypical causeOutlook
Musculoskeletal (functional) winging / dyskinesis The blade drifts or lifts because the muscles that stabilise it are weak, poorly conditioned, or uncoordinated S28 “Poor physical conditioning (strength and flexibility)” is a listed risk factor S27; “imbalance of your shoulder muscles” and “poor shoulder posture and mechanics” are listed shoulder-pain causes S1; posture changes shoulder-blade positioning S45 Usually addressed with scapular strengthening and postural work — this is the pattern a long slouched desk day plausibly feeds S28 S27 S45
Nerve-related winging A blade visibly protrudes because one of the controlling muscles is weakened/paralyzed Long thoracic nerve (serratus anterior), spinal accessory (trapezius), or dorsal scapular (rhomboids) nerve injury — from trauma, strain, or rarely unknown causes Needs a clinician; minor nerve cases can recover over months, severe/persistent cases may need further workup S29 S27

Physiopedia and physician-published clinical summaries note that scapular winging is most often linked to the serratus anterior/long thoracic nerve, can appear after acute trauma or micro-trauma, and that mild cases often respond to physical therapy and time S28 S27. A useful detail for interpreting your own “wing” pain: Physiopedia says severe, sleep-disturbing pain points to a neurological cause, whereas a winged scapula “due to muscular cause” is usually not painful or only moderately so — with any ache coming from “the strain and spasm of overcompensating periscapular muscles,” felt as dull aching and heaviness S28. (Physiopedia is a secondary, physiotherapist-edited reference; the underlying papers are listed on its page.) If the blade visibly sticks out, or you notice new arm/shoulder weakness, that's a “see a doctor” item, not a “try the exercises” item.

5. Red flags — don't self-treat these

🚨 Emergency (call 911): Sudden left shoulder pain with chest pressure or crushing pain, pain spreading to the left arm/jaw/neck, shortness of breath, dizziness, or sweating — a possible heart attack S1 S8.
Urgent care (same day) per Mayo Clinic: A shoulder that appears deformed after a fall; inability to use the shoulder or move the arm away from the body; intense pain; sudden swelling S7.
Schedule a doctor's visit if: Pain is getting worse or you're having a harder time moving the shoulder (Mayo Clinic S7); pain persists more than 2–4 weeks despite home treatment, or there is swelling, redness, or fever (MedlinePlus S1); pain that is characteristically worse at night — common with rotator cuff disease (S22); numbness, tingling or weakness traveling down the arm S10; or a new visible winging/weakness S28.

Note that some “shoulder pain” is actually referred pain from the neck, chest, or abdomen — it characteristically does not get worse when you move the shoulder S1 S8.
