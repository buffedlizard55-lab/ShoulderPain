Section 2 · Medical treatment

Treatment options, in order of escalation

The standard non-surgical pathway for shoulder pain is: activity modification and self-care → structured exercise/physical therapy → injections → (rarely) surgery. Below is each step with what trusted sources say it does — including where the evidence is weaker than it sounds.

Sources verified September 19, 2026

Before starting anything If any red flag on the Causes page applies (deformity, inability to move the arm, chest symptoms, fever with a hot red joint, progressive weakness, visible winging with weakness), skip to a clinician. Everything below assumes mild-to-moderate, non-urgent shoulder/upper-back pain.

Step 1 — Self-care (week 0–2)

1a. Protect and rest — but don't immobilize

MedlinePlus' rotator cuff self-care guidance: avoid activities that aggravate the shoulder (sports/overhead work until pain-free), but keep gentle motion; avoid full activity return until pain-free at rest and with movement (ideally confirmed by a provider or PT) S2. OSHA's desk guidance is the same idea at the workstation: vary postures, take micro-breaks, and alternate tasks so the overused muscles recover S16.

1b. Ice and heat — the protocol, from MedlinePlus

  • Ice: wrap in a clean towel (never directly on skin — frostbite risk), apply to the shoulder 20 minutes at a time, 3–4 times a day, to cut swelling when in pain S2.
  • Heat: moist heat (hot bath, shower, or heat pack) to loosen a painful/tight shoulder — useful before stretching or exercise S2 S25.

1c. Over-the-counter medication — with safety rules

  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) can reduce swelling and pain. If you take them daily, let your healthcare provider know so they can monitor your health S2.
  • MedlinePlus' ibuprofen and naproxen drug pages include the full precautions: take with food/water as directed, and be aware of serious risks (stomach bleeding/ulcers, kidney effects, cardiovascular and blood-pressure effects, interactions with other NSAIDs and blood thinners) S3 S4.
  • MedlinePlus' pain-reliever overview: NSAIDs are most useful when pain involves inflammation, and taking more than the recommended amount can cause nausea, stomach pain, or ulcers S6.
  • Topical diclofenac gel (an NSAID cream) is another option with a different safety profile (less whole-body exposure); MedlinePlus lists side effects and interaction warnings to check before use S5.
⚑ Flag for review I have not verified your personal medication history (blood pressure, stomach issues, other meds). A pharmacist is the right 5-minute check before starting regular NSAID use.

Step 2 — Exercise & physical therapy (the core treatment)

This is where the evidence is actually favorable — but with an honest asterisk:

  • A 2025 Cochrane review of manual therapy + exercise vs. placebo (inactive ultrasound) for chronic rotator cuff disease: pain improvement was “little or no different” from placebo; function improved 7.1 points more on a 100-point scale (≈7% absolute); 57% vs 41% self-reported treatment success; adverse events were relatively more frequent but mild — and the review notes the evidence base is limited (one high-quality trial for the main comparison) S22.
  • A 2020 synthesis of eight Cochrane reviews (published in PMC) concludes the opposite-leaning practical message: strengthening exercises, with or without manual therapy, were the interventions with the greatest treatment power over medium-to-long term — more than surgery or electrotherapy S24.
  • A 2023 randomized controlled trial (PubMed) of 33 patients with subacromial pain syndrome found a 12-week program of progressive scapular retraction exercises (± glenohumeral rotation) produced significant symptom reduction and greater acromiohumeral distance than standard care S23. This directly supports the scapular (“wing”) exercises on the Exercises page.
  • For frozen-shoulder-type stiffness, AAOS lists physical therapy (stretching/range-of-motion, sometimes with heat) as the primary treatment S25.
Honest interpretation No single treatment “fixes” mechanical shoulder pain instantly. Exercise is the option with the best long-term profile and the fewest downsides — and it targets the actual pattern (weak scapular stabilizers + tight chest from 12 hours of slouching). If pain isn't meaningfully better after ~2 weeks of consistent self-care + exercise, that's the trigger to book a clinician (Mayo Clinic lists “pain that is getting worse” or “harder time moving” as visit-worthwhile signs) S7.

A physical therapist adds value beyond the exercise sheet: they can confirm which pattern your winging follows (muscle weakness vs. nerve), demonstrate technique, and progress loads — especially important given the “wing” component S27 S28.

Step 3 — Steroid injection (when self-care + therapy isn't enough)

For bursitis and related shoulder inflammation, Mayo Clinic describes a corticosteroid shot into the shoulder bursa (often ultrasound-guided) as a treatment that “most often works quickly” to ease pain and make physical therapy easier — positioned alongside (not instead of) rest, ice, NSAIDs, and therapy S9. It's a prescription-level step: your clinician decides whether you're a candidate.

Step 4 — Surgery (last resort, specific diagnoses only)

Surgery enters the picture for confirmed structural problems (e.g., a significant rotator cuff tear) or when other treatments fail. AAOS notes frozen shoulder generally improves without surgery (though it can take up to ~3 years), and Mayo Clinic's bursitis pathway is nonsurgical first S25 S9. For true nerve-related scapular winging that doesn't recover, surgical options exist but are a specialist conversation S29. You are not in this column today — it's listed for completeness.

Step 5 — Supportive measures for the “wing” specifically

  • Targeted scapular strengthening (serratus anterior, trapezius, rhomboids) is the first-line conservative treatment for winging — see the dedicated routine on the Exercises page S27 S28.
  • Bracing/sling may be recommended by a clinician for several months in early nerve-related cases; Healthline (a lower-tier source) notes light PT or a brace early in recovery for some nerve injuries S29. Do not self-prescribe a brace — it can weaken the stabilizers if used inappropriately.
  • Activity modification: avoid the specific gym moves that aggravate (overhead pressing/heavy rowing with poor mechanics) until the pattern improves S2.
