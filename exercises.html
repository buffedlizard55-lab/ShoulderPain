Section 3 · Exercises

The exercise plan

A practical, sourced routine built from hospital and academic exercise programs. Each move links to the source that published it. Nothing here is a prescription — a physical therapist can tailor this to your specific pattern, which matters a lot with a “wing” component.

Sources verified September 19, 2026 (pass 2)

Rules for everything on this page Stay in a pain-free range — a gentle stretch is OK, sharp pain is a stop signal. Move slowly and control the motion — every cited program specifies slow, controlled movement with a slow return to the start position (e.g., the Bratton MD winging program S27). If pain, numbness, tingling, or new weakness appears, stop and see a clinician. Cleveland Clinic notes it takes about 4–6 weeks of consistent work to see real postural change S11.

Part A — Daily routine (≈10–12 min, no equipment)

Do this once or twice daily. Every movement below is taken from a published hospital/medical-center program.

Daily routine
#ExerciseHow (per source)DoseSource
1 Doorway pec stretch Stand in a doorway, upper arm parallel to the floor with elbow at 90°, palms on the frame; step one leg back and lean gently into the stretch of the upper chest. (Cleveland Clinic calls this the “doorway lunge”.) Hold ~30 s, 3 reps, each side S26 · S14
2 Scapular retraction (blade pinch) Arms at your sides, stand tall; pinch the shoulder blades together and down toward the spine (don't shrug toward the ears). Hold, then relax. This is the primary “anti-slouch” control drill. 10 reps × 2–3 sets, hold 5 s S26 · S11
3 Shoulder flexion stretch (table lean) Sit in a chair with your arm on a table; lean forward, sliding your arm forward on the table, until you feel a gentle stretch; hold, then return and repeat. (Physician-published winging program handout.) Hold 5–10 s, 5–10 reps, each side S27
4 Wall slides Stand close to a wall; slide your forearm and hand up the wall until you feel a comfortable stretch (step in for a deeper stretch). Keep shoulders relaxed. 10 reps × 2–3, each side S43
5 Chin tuck (“pull your head back”) Sitting or standing, pull your chin straight back (make a “double chin”) without looking up or down; hold. Reverses forward-head posture at the desk. Hold ~5–10 s, 10 reps S14
6 Wall angels Back against the wall; slowly slide arms out and up over your head with backs of hands touching the wall the whole way, then lower with control. 8–10 reps S14
7 Prone Y/T raises Lie face down (rolled towel under forehead); with thumbs up, lift arms a few inches into a “Y” or “T,” pinching shoulder blades; lower with control. (Start bodyweight — the hospital program adds light weights only when progressed.) 8–12 reps each letter × 2–3 S26
8 Crossover arm stretch Gently pull one arm across your chest just below chin level as far as comfortable without pain; hold. Hold 30 s, 3 reps, each side S25

Part B — Band work (2–3× per week, ~10 min)

Uses a light resistance band (see products). Slow tempo; elbows close to your ribs — Cleveland Clinic specifically warns not to pull elbows high, “as it can cause a pinch in your shoulders.”

Band routine (light resistance to start)
#ExerciseHow (per source)DoseSource
1 Standing row Anchor band at chest height; bend elbows, squeeze shoulder blades as you draw the band back, grazing your ribcage with your elbows. Return slowly. 10–15 reps × 3 sets S14 · S27
2 Resisted external rotation Elbow bent 90° at your side, upper arm supported; pinch shoulder blades and rotate the forearm out against the band, keeping the elbow at your side. 10–12 reps × 2–3, each arm S26
3 Band shoulder extension Arms extended forward; pinching shoulder blades, pull the arms down and backward — do not pull past your body's midline. 8–10 reps × 2–3 S27
4 Serratus “push-up plus” (wall version) Hands on a wall at shoulder height; without bending the arms, press your chest toward the wall until the shoulder blades squeeze together, then push back. Targets the serratus anterior — the muscle most implicated in winging. 10–15 reps × 2–3 S28
Why scapular work is the centerpiece A 2023 randomized trial found progressive scapular retraction exercises reduced symptoms in subacromial shoulder pain S23, and winging rehabilitation programs across clinical references consistently lead with serratus anterior + trapezius + rhomboid activation and retraction training S27 S28. That's exactly the muscle pattern a 12-hour slouch de-trains.

Part C — Wing-specific program (targeting the scapula)

If the “wing” feeling is the dominant symptom, the physician-published scapular winging program adds these control drills (all in the source's exercise set) S27:

  • Scapular retraction + external rotation — lie prone with the arm hanging over the bed edge, elbow bent 90°; rotate the arm toward the ceiling while pinching the shoulder blades together.
  • Scapular elevation (controlled shrugs) — stand in good posture, subtly raise the shoulders toward the ears, hold, then slowly lower.
  • Scapular protraction with overhead reach — arms straight ahead, squeeze the blades, then raise both arms overhead leading with the thumbs (“thumbs up”).
  • Scapular elevation in a good erect posture only — the program explicitly warns to avoid doing these in a slouched position.
⚑ Flag for review These drills are for functional (muscle-based) winging. If the blade visibly protrudes at rest, or overhead/pushing movements feel newly weak, stop Part C and get examined first — training around a true nerve palsy without diagnosis is the one scenario where self-treatment can be counterproductive S29 S28.

Part D — At the desk (the actual prevention)

The exercises only work if you interrupt the 12 hours of static load. OSHA's guidance: provide variation in postures, take short rest breaks (micro-breaks) where you stand, stretch and move, and alternate tasks S16. Cleveland Clinic's recommendation: walk around about once every hour if you can; even seated, vary your motion S11. For the eyes, the American Optometric Association endorses the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds S30.
