Section 3 · Exercises

The exercise plan

A practical, sourced routine built from hospital and academic exercise programs. Each move links to the source that published it. Nothing here is a prescription — a physical therapist can tailor this to your specific pattern, which matters a lot with a “wing” component.

Sources re-verified September 20, 2026 (pass 3 — doses re-checked line by line; pass 4 — AAOS sets/reps/days and the band-work dose re-read from the AAOS program page and confirmed)

Rules for everything on this page Stay in a pain-free range. The AAOS conditioning program puts it bluntly: “You should not feel pain during an exercise” S44; MedlinePlus adds that if you have pain during or after an exercise “you may need to change the way you are doing the exercise or lower the resistance,” and that you should ask a provider or physical therapist to check your form before doing them at home S46. Move slowly and control the motion — every cited program specifies slow, controlled movement with a slow return to the start position (e.g., the Bratton MD winging program S27). AAOS: warm up 5–10 minutes (walk, stationary bike), stretch before and after the strengthening work, run the program for 4–6 weeks, then keep it up 2–3 days a week for maintenance S44. If pain, numbness, tingling, or new weakness appears, stop and see a clinician. Cleveland Clinic notes it takes about 4–6 weeks of consistent work to see real postural change S11.

Official illustrated programs (the primary references — open these for the pictures)

Every exercise below comes from one of these published programs. None of the verified official pages publishes a video, so this project links the illustrated handouts rather than third-party videos (no unverified video links are included — see Next Steps).

  • AAOS OrthoInfo — Rotator Cuff and Shoulder Conditioning Program (American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons; written/reviewed by American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons members). 18 illustrated exercises with sets, reps and days-per-week; also available as a printable PDF handout S44.
  • MedlinePlus (NIH) — Rotator cuff exercises: the official list of stretches (posterior stretch, pendulum, wall stretches) and strengthening moves (band internal/external rotation, isometrics, wall push-ups, scapular retraction with and without tubing, arm reach), reviewed 6/4/2025 S46.
  • Texas Children's Hospital — Exercises for Shoulders (PDF handout) S26; Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center — Exercises for Frozen Shoulder (PDF) S43; Bratton MD — Scapular Winging program (physician-published PDF) S27; Cleveland Clinic — posture exercises S14.

† In the tables below, a dose marked † is a conservative default because the cited source gives no rep count or hold time for that move. Unmarked doses are quoted from the cited source.

Part A — Daily routine (≈10–12 min, no equipment)

Do this once or twice daily. Every movement below is taken from a published hospital/medical-center program.

Daily routine

1 Doorway pec stretch Stand in a doorway, upper arm parallel to the floor with elbow at 90°, palms on the frame; step one leg back and lean gently into the stretch of the upper chest. (Cleveland Clinic calls this the “doorway lunge”.) Hold 20–30 s, 2–3 reps each side (the winging program's stretches use 20–30 s holds; Cleveland Clinic's version holds only “a few seconds” — either is acceptable) S26 · S14 · S27
2 Scapular retraction (blade pinch) Arms at your sides, stand tall; pinch the shoulder blades together and down toward the spine (don't shrug toward the ears). Hold, then relax. This is the primary “anti-slouch” control drill. Cleveland Clinic's desk version: “pinch your shoulder blades together 10 times.” AAOS's lying-down version (“scapula setting”): draw the blades together and down, ease off halfway, hold 10 s, 10 reps. 10 reps, hold 5–10 s; 2–3 sets† S13 · S44 · S26
3 Shoulder flexion stretch (table lean) Sit in a chair with your arm on a table; lean forward, sliding your arm forward on the table, until you feel a gentle stretch; hold, then return and repeat. (Physician-published winging program handout.) Hold 5–10 s, 5–10 reps, each side S27
4 Wall slides Stand close to a wall; slide your forearm and hand up the wall until you feel a comfortable stretch (step in for a deeper stretch). Keep shoulders relaxed. 10 reps × 2–3, each side S43
5 Chin tuck (“pull your head back”) Sitting or standing, pull your chin straight back (make a “double chin”) without looking up or down; hold “for several seconds” (source wording). Reverses forward-head posture at the desk. Hold several seconds; 10 reps† S14
6 Wall angels Back against the wall; slowly slide arms out and up over your head with backs of hands touching the wall the whole way, then lower with control. 8–10 reps† S14
7 Prone Y/T raises (AAOS's close cousin is “bent-over horizontal abduction”: lying face down, arm hanging, raise the straight arm to eye level and lower slowly) Lie face down (rolled towel under forehead); with thumbs up, lift arms a few inches into a “Y” or “T,” pinching shoulder blades; lower with control. Start with no weight — the hospital handout leaves the weight blank for the therapist to fill in; AAOS progresses its version from a weight that allows 3 sets of 8 up to 3 sets of 12, adding 1 lb at a time to a maximum of 5 lb. Bodyweight, in the daily routine: 8–12 reps each letter × 2†. If you add a weight: 3 sets of 8 → 3 sets of 12, 3 days/week (AAOS dosing) S26 · S44
8 Crossover arm stretch Relax the shoulders and gently pull one arm across your chest as far as possible without pain, holding at the upper arm (not the elbow); you should feel it at the back of the shoulder. Hold 30 s, relax 30 s; 4 reps each side, 5–6 days/week (AAOS); the frozen-shoulder page says to ask your PT for reps S44 · S25
9 Passive external rotation stretch (stick) — optional, if the shoulder feels tight turning outward Hold a light stick (yardstick) in both hands; keep the elbow of the sore side against your body and push the stick horizontally with the other hand until you feel a pull without pain, hips facing forward. Hold 30 s, relax 30 s; 4 reps each side, 5–6 days/week S44

Part B — Band work (2–3× per week, ~10 min)

Uses a light resistance band (see products). Slow tempo; elbows close to your ribs — Cleveland Clinic specifically warns not to pull elbows high, “as it can cause a pinch in your shoulders” S14. MedlinePlus sets the loading rule: “Rotator cuff muscles are the small muscles and you should focus your exercises on lower weights and higher repetitions. Most of the exercises may focus on your shoulder blade” S2. AAOS's band set-up: make a 3-foot loop, tie the ends, anchor it to a doorknob; start at 3 sets of 8 and progress to 3 sets of 12 as it gets easier, 3 days per week S44.

3 Resisted internal rotation Same set-up; elbow pressed into your side, bring the forearm across your body against the band; return slowly. (AAOS pairs internal with external rotation; MedlinePlus lists both.) 3 sets of 8 → 3 sets of 12; 3 days/week, each arm S44 · S46
4 Band shoulder extension Arms extended forward; pinching shoulder blades, pull the arms down and backward — do not pull past your body's midline. 8–10 reps × 2–3† S27
5 Serratus “push-up plus” (wall version) Hands on a wall at shoulder height; without bending the arms, let the chest sink toward the wall until the shoulder blades squeeze together, then push the wall away until the blades spread apart (the “plus”). The winging program includes a wall protraction push; Physiopedia lists push-up-plus variants; MedlinePlus lists wall push-ups among rotator-cuff exercises. Targets the serratus anterior — the muscle most implicated in winging. 10–15 reps × 2–3† S27 · S28 · S46

Not included by default: AAOS's sleeper stretch is performed lying on the affected shoulder — for a left-side sleeper with a sore left shoulder, that is the exact position under suspicion, so leave it to a PT to prescribe S44.

Why scapular work is the centerpiece A 2023 randomized trial found a progressive scapular-stabilisation program (retraction, with or without added glenohumeral rotation work) reduced symptoms in subacromial shoulder pain S23; a synthesis of Cochrane reviews rates specific exercise over non-specific exercise as high-quality evidence S24; and winging rehabilitation programs across clinical references consistently lead with serratus anterior + trapezius + rhomboid activation and retraction training S27 S28. Mayo Clinic Health System names the same target: the large shoulder and back muscles must support the small rotator cuff, and prolonged sitting leaves them weaker than the front of the body S45.

Part C — Wing-specific program (targeting the scapula)

If the “wing” feeling is the dominant symptom, the physician-published scapular winging program adds these control drills (all in the source's exercise set) S27:

  • Scapular retraction + external rotation — lie prone with the arm hanging over the bed edge, elbow bent 90°; rotate the arm toward the ceiling while pinching the shoulder blades together.
  • Scapular elevation (controlled shrugs) — stand in good posture, subtly raise the shoulders toward the ears, hold, then slowly lower.
  • Scapular retraction and elevation (the program's name for it — an earlier version of this page mislabelled it “protraction”) — arms straight ahead, squeeze the blades, then raise both arms overhead leading with the thumbs (“thumbs up”).
  • Scapular elevation in a good erect posture only — the program explicitly warns to avoid doing these in a slouched position.
⚑ Flag for review These drills are for functional (muscle-based) winging. If the blade visibly protrudes at rest, or overhead/pushing movements feel newly weak, stop Part C and get examined first — training around a true nerve palsy without diagnosis is the one scenario where self-treatment can be counterproductive S29 S28.

Part D — At the desk (the actual prevention)

The exercises only work if you interrupt the 12 hours of static load. OSHA's guidance: provide variation in postures, take short rest breaks (micro-breaks) where you stand, stretch and move, and alternate tasks S16. Cleveland Clinic's recommendation: walk around about once every hour if you can; even seated, vary your motion S11. For the eyes, the American Optometric Association endorses the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds S30.
