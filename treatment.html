Section 2 · Treatment pathway

What to do, in a safe order

For non-emergency shoulder pain, a sensible pathway is to reduce aggravation, keep tolerable motion, build a diagnosis-appropriate exercise plan, and escalate when the trend or examination calls for it. This page is not a prescription and cannot rule out a tear or nerve problem.

Medical claims and safety wording reviewed September 20, 2026. Medication doses are intentionally omitted: use the product label, pharmacist, or clinician.

Do not start with self-treatment if… You have chest symptoms, sudden severe pain, a deformed shoulder, sudden swelling, inability to move/use the arm, progressive weakness, new visible winging, fever with a hot/red joint, or concerning numbness/tingling. Use the red-flag section and seek the appropriate level of care. S1 S7 S28

Step 1 — Remove the aggravator without freezing the shoulder

For a mild, non-urgent pattern, temporarily reduce the movement, load, or position that clearly increases pain. MedlinePlus advises avoiding prolonged work with the arms above shoulder level, carrying objects close to the body, taking regular breaks from repetitive activity, and returning to ordinary activity gradually. It also says not to play sports until there is no pain during rest or activity. S2

“Rest” does not mean holding the arm still all day. MedlinePlus describes rehabilitation beginning with passive range of motion in some rotator-cuff patterns; its general shoulder-pain guidance says to rest for the next few days and then slowly return to regular activities. Use comfortable motion, not forceful stretching or painful testing. S1 S2

  • Pause heavy overhead pressing, dips, painful rows, and any lift that produces sharp pain, loss of control, or increasing weakness.
  • Change the desk position and alternate tasks rather than trying to “sit perfectly” for 12 hours. OSHA recommends posture variation, micro-breaks, and task alternation. S16
  • Keep the painful shoulder off the mattress while it is irritable; MedlinePlus suggests the pain-free side or back sleeping. S2

Step 2 — Ice or heat according to the source and the response

  • Ice: MedlinePlus' rotator-cuff instructions say to wrap ice in a clean towel and apply it for 20 minutes at a time, 3–4 times daily. Never put ice directly on the skin. S2
  • Heat: the same source says moist heat such as a hot bath, shower, or heat pack may loosen a painful/tight shoulder. It can be a reasonable comfort measure before tolerable movement, but stop if it worsens swelling or pain. S2

Do not mix timings from unrelated conditions into a universal rule. The timing above is the rotator-cuff self-care instruction; a clinician may advise differently for your diagnosis, skin sensation, circulation, or injury.

Step 3 — Medication safety, not medication shopping

Over-the-counter medicines can be useful for some people, but they do not identify or repair the cause. MedlinePlus says each pain medicine has benefits and risks; NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen can help mild-to-moderate pain with inflammation but excess use can cause stomach pain, nausea, or ulcers. Step 4 — Physical therapy and a diagnosis-appropriate exercise plan

PT is the main conservative route when symptoms persist, recur, or include a scapular movement problem. AAOS says many scapular disorders are treated nonsurgically with physical therapy, posture work, stretching, and strengthening. The NCBI clinical review says evaluation may need to distinguish muscle coordination from nerve dysfunction and may include EMG/nerve-conduction testing when appropriate. S27 S28

The evidence is useful but not a guarantee. A Cochrane review of manual therapy plus exercise for rotator-cuff disease searched through March 2015 and found little or no difference in overall pain versus placebo in its main comparison, with a small function improvement and limited trial evidence. It should not be represented as current, diagnosis-universal proof that one exercise method is superior. S22

A synthesis of Cochrane reviews found strengthening exercise, with or without manual therapy, to be among the most useful medium- and long-term approaches in the conditions reviewed; it also reported high-quality evidence favoring specific over nonspecific exercises in one comparison. A 2023 randomized trial in subacromial pain found improvement in both supervised programs and additional benefits for one group adding gradually elevated glenohumeral rotation. Those results support progressive, supervised, diagnosis-matched work — not a universal prescription for visible winging. S24 S23

What a PT visit can add A clinician can observe the scapula during a wall push and arm raise, test strength and range, check the neck/nerve contribution, choose an appropriate load, and progress or stop exercises. That matters more than copying a “winging workout” from the internet. S27 S28

Step 5 — Injection only for a clinician-confirmed indication

Mayo Clinic describes a corticosteroid injection into the bursa as an option for bursitis that can work quickly and make physical therapy easier. It is an adjunct to the diagnosis and rehabilitation plan, not a way to confirm or permanently fix a “wing.” A clinician decides whether an injection fits the cause, timing, risks, and alternatives. S9

Step 6 — Imaging, specialist care, or surgery when the examination supports it

Tests are selected for the suspected problem. Mass General Brigham lists X-ray, MRI/CT, EMG, and other tests among possible shoulder evaluations; NCBI specifically discusses EMG/nerve-conduction testing for suspected scapular-winging nerve dysfunction. Neither a screenshot nor a home wall push can replace that assessment. S10 S28

Surgery is not a default endpoint for desk-related pain. It may be discussed for a confirmed structural tear, serious injury, or persistent nerve-related winging that fails appropriate conservative care. AAOS notes that many frozen shoulders improve without surgery and Mayo presents nonsurgical bursitis care first. S25 S9
