Section 2 · Medical treatment

Treatment options, in order of escalation

The standard non-surgical pathway for shoulder pain is: activity modification and self-care → structured exercise/physical therapy → injections → (rarely) surgery. Below is each step with what trusted sources say it does — including where the evidence is weaker than it sounds.

Sources re-verified September 20, 2026 (pass 3 — every citation on this page re-opened)

Before starting anything If any red flag on the Causes page applies (deformity, inability to move the arm, chest symptoms, fever with a hot red joint, progressive weakness, visible winging with weakness), skip to a clinician. Everything below assumes mild-to-moderate, non-urgent shoulder/upper-back pain.

Step 1 — Self-care (week 0–2)

1a. Protect and rest — but don't immobilize

MedlinePlus' rotator cuff self-care guidance, in its own words: “do not work with your arms above shoulder level for a long time”; “lift and carry objects close to your body”; “take regular breaks from any activity you do over and over again”; “it is best to avoid playing sports until you have no pain during rest or activity”; and when you sit, “keep your head over your shoulder and place a towel or pillow behind your lower back” with your feet flat on the floor or on a footstool S2. The same page says rehab usually starts with passive range-of-motion (using the good arm to move the sore one) rather than total rest, and the general shoulder-pain article says to rest “for the next few days” and then “slowly return to your regular activities” — if you're recovering from tendinitis, keep doing range-of-motion exercises to avoid a frozen shoulder S1. OSHA's desk guidance is the same idea at the workstation: vary postures, take micro-breaks, and alternate tasks so the overused muscles recover S16.

1b. Ice and heat — the protocol, from MedlinePlus

  • Ice: wrap in a clean towel (never directly on skin — frostbite risk), apply to the shoulder 20 minutes at a time, 3–4 times a day, to cut swelling when in pain S2.
  • Heat: moist heat (hot bath, shower, or heat pack) to loosen a painful/tight shoulder — useful before stretching or exercise S2 S25.

1c. Over-the-counter medication — with safety rules

  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) can reduce swelling and pain. If you take them daily, let your healthcare provider know so they can monitor your health S2.
  • MedlinePlus' ibuprofen and naproxen drug pages include the full precautions: take with food/water as directed, and be aware of serious risks (stomach bleeding/ulcers, kidney effects, cardiovascular and blood-pressure effects, interactions with other NSAIDs and blood thinners) S3 S4.
  • MedlinePlus' pain-reliever overview: NSAIDs are most useful when pain involves inflammation, and taking more than the recommended amount can cause nausea, stomach pain, or ulcers S6.
  • Topical creams and gels. Mayo Clinic's first self-care suggestion for minor shoulder pain is a pain reliever applied to the skin — a cream or gel containing 10% menthol, or the NSAID diclofenac (e.g., Voltaren) S8. Read the MedlinePlus diclofenac-gel page before choosing it: the over-the-counter gel carries the same boxed heart-attack/stroke and stomach-bleeding warnings as oral NSAIDs, its labelled uses are arthritis pain in the hands, wrists, elbows, feet, ankles and knees (the page says “do not apply to any area your doctor did not tell you to treat”), and it is limited to two body areas, four times a day, for no more than 21 days without a doctor S5. In plain terms: ask a pharmacist or doctor before using diclofenac gel on a shoulder, and don't stack it with oral ibuprofen/naproxen.
⚑ Flags for review (1) I have not verified your personal medication history (blood pressure, stomach issues, other meds). A pharmacist is the right 5-minute check before starting regular NSAID use. (2) Pass 3 correction: an earlier version of this page described topical diclofenac as having “less whole-body exposure” — that claim is not made by the cited MedlinePlus page and has been removed.

Step 2 — Exercise & physical therapy (the core treatment)

This is where the evidence is actually favorable — but with an honest asterisk:

  • A 2016 Cochrane review (Page et al.; findings re-verified Sep 2026) of manual therapy + exercise vs. placebo (inactive ultrasound) for chronic rotator cuff disease: pain improvement was “little or no different” from placebo; function improved 7.1 points more on a 100-point scale (≈7% absolute); 57% vs 41% self-reported treatment success; adverse events were relatively more frequent but mild — and the review notes the evidence base is limited (one high-quality trial for the main comparison; trials searched up to March 2015). Its lower-quality comparisons are just as useful for expectations: manual therapy + exercise showed “little or no difference” in pain and function versus a steroid injection, and versus arthroscopic subacromial decompression surgery S22.
  • A 2020 synthesis of Cochrane reviews (31 Cochrane reviews screened, 8 eligible; published in the São Paulo Medical Journal and archived in PMC) concludes the practical message: strengthening exercises, with or without manual therapy, were the interventions with the greatest treatment power over the medium and long term — greater than surgical procedures, electrotherapy or photobiomodulation. Its evidence table also rates “specific exercises versus nonspecific exercises” as high-quality evidence favouring the specific (targeted) program at 3 months, and “exercise versus subacromial decompression” as no difference at 6 and 12 months S24.
  • A 2023 randomized controlled trial (PubMed, double-blind, n = 33) in subacromial pain syndrome: both groups did a 12-week supervised program (manual therapy, stretching and progressive scapular stabilisation/retraction exercises); the group that also added glenohumeral rotation exercises at gradual elevation angles gained significantly more acromiohumeral distance and better disability (SPADI) and activity-pain scores, while both groups improved significantly over time. Night pain and rest pain did not differ between groups S23. This directly supports the scapular (“wing”) exercises on the Exercises page.
  • For frozen-shoulder-type stiffness, AAOS lists physical therapy (stretching/range-of-motion, sometimes with heat) as the primary treatment S25.
Honest interpretation No single treatment “fixes” mechanical shoulder pain instantly. Exercise is the option with the best long-term profile and the fewest downsides — and it targets the actual pattern (weak scapular stabilizers + tight chest from 12 hours of slouching). MedlinePlus says to contact a provider if pain persists more than 2–4 weeks despite home treatment, and Mayo Clinic lists “pain that is getting worse” or “a harder time moving” as visit-worthwhile signs — that's the trigger to book a clinician S1 S7. The Pain Log page makes this checkpoint measurable.

A physical therapist adds value beyond the exercise sheet: they can confirm which pattern your winging follows (muscle weakness vs. nerve), demonstrate technique, and progress loads — especially important given the “wing” component S27 S28.

Step 3 — Steroid injection (when self-care + therapy isn't enough)

For bursitis and related shoulder inflammation, Mayo Clinic describes a corticosteroid shot into the shoulder bursa (often ultrasound-guided) as a treatment that “most often works quickly” to ease pain and make physical therapy easier — positioned alongside (not instead of) rest, ice, NSAIDs, and therapy S9. It's a prescription-level step: your clinician decides whether you're a candidate.

Step 4 — Surgery (last resort, specific diagnoses only)

Surgery enters the picture for confirmed structural problems (e.g., a significant rotator cuff tear) or when other treatments fail. AAOS notes frozen shoulder generally improves without surgery (though it can take up to ~3 years), and Mayo Clinic's bursitis pathway is nonsurgical first S25 S9. The Cochrane review adds two useful data points: exercise alone gave “less improvement in overall pain, but not function” than surgical repair for a rotator cuff tear, while exercise showed little or no difference from arthroscopic subacromial decompression — the operation once common for impingement-type pain S22. For true nerve-related scapular winging that doesn't recover, surgical options exist but are a specialist conversation S29. You are not in this column today — it's listed for completeness.

Step 5 — Supportive measures for the “wing” specifically

  • Targeted scapular strengthening (serratus anterior, trapezius, rhomboids) is the first-line conservative treatment for winging — see the dedicated routine on the Exercises page S27 S28.
  • Bracing/sling may be recommended by a clinician for several months in early nerve-related cases; Healthline (a lower-tier source) notes light PT or a brace early in recovery for some nerve injuries S29. Do not self-prescribe a brace — it can weaken the stabilizers if used inappropriately.
  • Activity modification: avoid the specific gym moves that aggravate (overhead pressing/heavy rowing with poor mechanics) until the pattern improves S2.
